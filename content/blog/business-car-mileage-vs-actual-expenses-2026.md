For an eligible US business-car deduction, compare **standard mileage with actual expenses**, but check eligibility before choosing the larger number. In 2026 the business mileage rate changes midway through the year: **72.5 cents per mile before July 1 and 76 cents per mile from July 1**. One rate multiplied by a whole year's driving gives the wrong result when trips span both periods.

These are US federal rules. For a nonresident owner of a single-member disregarded LLC, first confirm that the deduction relates to the relevant US taxable business income. [Publication 519](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p519) describes that connection. This is not a UK mileage allowance or a VAT calculation.

## Use the rate for the correct 2026 period

| Business travel period | Rate in US dollars per mile | Primary authority |
|---|---|---|
| January 1–June 30, 2026 | $0.725 | [IRS Notice 2026-10](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2026-04_IRB) |
| July 1–December 31, 2026 | $0.76 | [IRS Announcement 2026-11](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2026-29_irb) |

The July announcement modifies the earlier notice. Keep travel dates in the log, not just an annual mileage total. The IRS also maintains a [current and historical mileage-rate table](https://www.irs.gov/tax-professionals/standard-mileage-rates).

For the standard method, start with qualifying business miles for each period. For the actual method, start with eligible vehicle costs and allocate business use. Business parking and tolls can be separately deductible under either method. See [IRS Topic 510](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc510).

## A worked comparison with both rates

This hypothetical example assumes an owned vehicle eligible for either method and an otherwise allowable US business deduction. It excludes commuting and private trips from business mileage.

| Input | Illustrative amount |
|---|---|
| Business miles, January–June | 4,000 miles |
| Business miles, July–December | 6,000 miles |
| Total vehicle mileage for the year | 16,000 miles |
| Eligible actual vehicle costs before allocation | $9,000 |
| Separate qualifying business parking and tolls | $180 |

The **standard mileage calculation** is:

**(4,000 × $0.725) + (6,000 × $0.76) = $2,900 + $4,560 = $7,460.**

Adding $180 of qualifying parking and tolls gives **$7,640**.

The **actual-expense calculation** uses a business ratio of **10,000 ÷ 16,000 = 62.5%**. The allocated costs are **$9,000 × 62.5% = $5,625**. Adding the same $180 gives **$5,805**.

Standard mileage is **$1,835 larger in this example**. That is a deduction comparison, not $1,835 of tax saved. Actual tax savings depend on the taxpayer's tax position. The $9,000 input assumes applicable cost-recovery limits have already been addressed; it is not a car's purchase price or an unreviewed bank-spending total.

## The larger result is only useful if the method is available

For an owned car, standard mileage must be chosen in the first year the car is available for business use to preserve that route in later years. Prior MACRS, section 179 or special depreciation claims can disqualify standard mileage. Operating five or more cars simultaneously also prevents its use. For a leased car, choosing standard mileage commits you to that method for the whole lease, including renewals. These restrictions are in [IRS Topic 510](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc510).

Ask for last year's return and the vehicle's depreciation schedule before comparing methods. A spreadsheet cannot restore an option that an earlier election removed.

Using standard mileage also means you cannot add the same year's actual operating costs again. [Publication 463, car expenses](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p463) explains the method and depreciation interaction. Fuel or charging receipts can still support other records, but they do not become an extra operating-cost deduction on top of mileage.

## Keep a log that can answer follow-up questions

A useful log records the date, start and destination, business purpose, miles and vehicle identifier. Preserve annual odometer readings and separate personal and commuting trips. Add a receipt reference where there is parking, a toll or another separately reviewed cost.

“Client work: 600 miles” is difficult to investigate. “July 8: office to client's premises for an installation meeting; 32 miles; parking receipt P-18” gives the preparer something to trace. Those entries are editorial examples, not IRS-approved wording.

For actual costs, reconcile receipts to payments and flag purchase costs, financing, reimbursements and disposal proceeds rather than treating every vehicle-related debit as a running expense. When a receipt covers several cars, identify the vehicle before allocating it.

## Keep reimbursements out of the mileage arithmetic

An owner paying a cost personally and an LLC reimbursing that owner are payment events. The mileage calculation measures an eligible expense. They should not be substituted for one another in a Form 5472 total.

Use the [owner-funding and reimbursement guide](/blog/form-5472-owner-loans-contributions-reimbursements) to assemble the actual transfer trail, alongside the [company-car deduction guide](/blog/company-car-deductions-foreign-owned-us-llc). The foreign-owned LLC's information return remains separate from any income-tax deduction claim.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I use 76 cents for all 2026 business miles?

No. The revised rate applies from July 1, 2026. The earlier period uses 72.5 cents, subject to the applicable eligibility rules.

### Does a bigger deduction mean the business should buy a more expensive car?

No. Compare the cash cost, practical business need and available methods. A deduction reduces eligible taxable income; it does not repay the full cost of the vehicle.
