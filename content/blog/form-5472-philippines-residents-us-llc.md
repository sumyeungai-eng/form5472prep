**If you live in the Philippines and own a US single-member LLC, you must file Form 5472 with a pro forma Form 1120 for each year the LLC had a reportable transaction with you. A US–Philippines income tax treaty exists, but it changes nothing about this filing. If the BIR has issued you a TIN, that number normally goes in the FTIN box.**

Filipino freelancers, virtual assistants, agency owners and ecommerce sellers form Wyoming, Delaware and New Mexico LLCs for practical reasons: US clients want to pay a US company, and US payment processors and banks are easier to reach through one. Foreign nationals based in Manila, Cebu or Siargao often keep a US LLC for the same reasons.

The Philippine position differs from a UK or Indian owner's in two ways. There is a treaty on the IRS list, and the Bureau of Internal Revenue (BIR) issues TINs to individuals. Both affect how you fill in the form. Neither removes the need to file it, and neither turns a BIR filing into a substitute for the US one.

## Do Philippines residents have to file Form 5472?

Yes. The test is the same as for any other non-US owner, and it has three conditions:

1. The LLC is a US disregarded entity, meaning a single-member LLC that has not elected to be taxed as a corporation.
2. Its sole member is a non-US person. A Philippines resident who is not a US citizen, green card holder or US tax resident meets this condition.
3. At least one **reportable transaction** took place during the tax year between the LLC and its owner or another foreign related party.

Treasury Regulation § 1.6038A-1 treats a foreign-owned US disregarded entity as a corporation separate from its owner, but **solely** for the § 6038A reporting rules. That treatment applies to tax years beginning on or after 1 January 2017. It depends on where the owner is and what the owner's status is. It does not depend on how much tax you pay anywhere.

Reportable transactions are money, property or services moving **between you and your own LLC**. That covers contributions in, distributions out, loans in either direction, and payments for goods or services between you and the LLC. **Customer revenue is not reportable.** A Stripe or Wise payout from a US client stays off the form. Sending that money from the LLC's account to your personal BDO or BPI peso account is reportable.

For a typical Philippines-based owner, that means:

- Pesos you converted and sent to fund the LLC's US account are a **reportable contribution**.
- Every transfer from the LLC to your personal Philippine account is a **reportable distribution**.
- Your own money used to pay the LLC's software or ad bills is **reportable**.
- A payment from the LLC to a Philippine corporation you also own is **reportable, and that corporation is a second related party**.

The IRS instructions state that a **$25,000** penalty may apply for failure to file when due and in the prescribed manner, for filing a substantially incomplete Form 5472, or for failure to keep the required records. A substantially incomplete form counts as a failure to file. The penalty is not automatic for every error, but it is the reason the form needs to be complete.

## Does the US–Philippines tax treaty change anything?

**The Philippines is on the IRS [United States Income Tax Treaties – A to Z](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/international-businesses/united-states-income-tax-treaties-a-to-z) list.** It sits in the P section between Pakistan and Poland. The IRS [Philippines tax treaty documents page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/international-businesses/philippines-tax-treaty-documents) links to the 1976 income tax treaty and its technical explanation.

A treaty decides which country may tax which income, and it can lower US withholding on some US-source payments to a qualifying Philippine resident. Whether a treaty article applies to your income is a separate question with its own filings, and it is worth asking a professional.

**The treaty changes nothing about Form 5472.** Form 5472 is an information return required by IRC § 6038A, not a tax. No treaty article exempts a disregarded entity from it. Owners in treaty countries file on exactly the same terms as owners in countries with no treaty.

## What goes in the FTIN box if you have a BIR TIN?

In the FTIN field on line 4b(3), enter the taxpayer identification number issued by the country where you are resident for tax purposes. For many Philippines-resident owners, that is the TIN the BIR has already issued to them.

The BIR's Citizen's Charter pages describe several routes to a TIN. Local employees, including foreign-national employees, are issued one on registration. The BIR's [E.O. 98 and non-resident applicant page](https://www.bir.gov.ph/BIRCC-RDO-External-Service-04) covers people who need a TIN to deal with government agencies, non-resident Filipino citizens, overseas Filipino workers, and foreign nationals who need one for Philippine work permits. Self-employed individuals and professionals register with the BIR through a separate process.

| Your situation | FTIN field (line 4b(3)) | Reference ID (line 4b(2)) |
|---|---|---|
| Philippine tax resident with a BIR TIN | Your BIR TIN | Needed only if line 4b(1) is blank; keep it the same every year |
| Philippines-based but tax resident in another country | The TIN from that country | As above |
| No tax identification number from any country | "None" or "N/A", not blank | Required; use a self-assigned ID and keep it the same every year |
| You already hold a US SSN or ITIN | Your FTIN, if you have one | Not needed; line 4b(1) carries the US number |

Three mistakes come up again and again. The first is entering a PhilSys (PhilID) number, passport number or driver's licence number in the FTIN field: those identify you, but they are not tax numbers. The second is entering the TIN of a Philippine corporation you own, which belongs to that company and not to you. The third is applying for a US ITIN only to fill line 4b. The reference ID exists so that owners can file without one. See our [FTIN and reference ID guide](/blog/form-5472-ftin-reference-id-foreign-address) and [do I need an ITIN for Form 5472](/blog/itin-required-form-5472).

## Does Philippine tax affect the US filing?

It does not. Philippine tax is still worth understanding, because it is what decides whether you owe anything locally. The rules below are an outline only.

The BIR publishes the [National Internal Revenue Code](https://www.bir.gov.ph/tax-code) on its own site. Section 22 defines a **resident alien** as a non-citizen whose residence is within the Philippines, and sets out when a Filipino citizen is treated as a **nonresident citizen**. Section 23 then sets the scope of tax. A Philippine citizen residing in the Philippines is taxable on income from sources within and outside the Philippines. A nonresident citizen, and any alien individual, whether resident or not, is taxable only on income from Philippine sources.

The Philippine rule is not a flat 183-day residence test of the kind nomads discuss. Section 25(A)(1) deems a nonresident alien who stays in the Philippines for an aggregate of **more than 180 days** in a calendar year to be a "nonresident alien doing business in the Philippines". Everything else turns on residence and citizenship as the Code defines them.

This table is the core of the post. It separates what the BIR may expect from what the IRS expects:

| Obligation | Filed with | What it covers | Does it satisfy the other side? |
|---|---|---|---|
| TIN registration | BIR | Gives you, the individual, a Philippine taxpayer number | No. A BIR TIN goes **into** Form 5472; it is not a US filing |
| Registration as self-employed or professional | BIR | Your own business activity in the Philippines | No. Registering locally does not register or report the US LLC to the IRS |
| Philippine annual income tax return, where one is required | BIR | Your taxable income under the Philippine Tax Code | No. It does not report owner–LLC transactions to the IRS |
| Form 5472 plus pro forma Form 1120 | IRS | Reportable transactions between the US LLC and you or other foreign related parties | No. It reports no Philippine income and settles nothing with the BIR |
| Form 7004, if you need more time | IRS | An extension for the US information return only | No. It extends no BIR deadline |

Whether you personally need to register, which return you file, and how your LLC's profit is treated in the Philippines are questions for the BIR or a Philippine tax adviser. We do not advise on Philippine tax.

## How does a Philippines-based owner actually file?

Form 5472 cannot be e-filed, and no consumer tax software supports it.

1. **Prepare the pro forma Form 1120.** Write **"Foreign-owned U.S. DE"** across the top of page 1. Only the LLC's name and address and items B and E are required.
2. **Prepare Form 5472.** Part I is for the LLC. Part II is for you, with your Philippine address, your FTIN or "None", and a reference ID where needed. Then complete the related-party and transaction parts, and attach a statement itemising contributions and distributions.
3. **Convert pesos to US dollars** at a reasonable rate for each transaction date, apply the same method all year, and state your basis. See our [currency conversion guide](/blog/form-5472-currency-conversion-exchange-rates).
4. **Sign the return and fax it to 855-887-7737**, or mail it to Internal Revenue Service, 1973 Rulon White Blvd, M/S 6112, Attn: PIN Unit, Ogden, UT 84201.
5. **Keep the timestamped fax receipt.** It proves that you sent the fax. It does not show that the IRS accepted the return.

For a calendar-year LLC, the regular due date is generally **April 15**. Form 7004 extends it, using the special instructions for foreign-owned disregarded entities. See our [deadline guide](/blog/form-5472-deadline-2026).

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to file Form 5472 if my LLC's clients are all outside the Philippines?

Yes, if money moved between you and the LLC during the year. Where your customers are does not matter. Customer payments are not reportable, but your contributions to the LLC and your withdrawals from it are.

### Does the US–Philippines treaty exempt me from Form 5472?

No. The Philippines is on the IRS treaty list, but treaties decide which country taxes which income. Form 5472 is an information return, and no treaty removes it.

### Should I put my BIR TIN on Form 5472?

If the Philippines is your country of tax residence and the BIR has issued you a TIN, enter it in the FTIN field on line 4b(3). Do not enter a company's TIN or a PhilID number.

### I am a foreigner living in the Philippines with no TIN. What goes in the FTIN box?

Enter the TIN from your country of tax residence if you have one. If you have none, write "None" or "N/A" and put a self-assigned reference ID on line 4b(2), using the same ID every year.

### Does filing with the BIR count as filing Form 5472?

No. The BIR and the IRS are separate authorities with separate returns. A Philippine return does not report owner–LLC transactions to the IRS, and Form 5472 settles nothing with the BIR.

### Can I get a Philippine digital nomad visa?

We could not confirm from a Philippine government source that applications are open. The Bureau of Immigration's visa page lists no such category as of 18 September 2026. Check the Bureau of Immigration or the DFA directly.

### I have never filed and my LLC is several years old. What now?

File every outstanding year as soon as you can, with a reasonable cause statement, ideally before the IRS contacts you. Each year carries its own penalty exposure.
