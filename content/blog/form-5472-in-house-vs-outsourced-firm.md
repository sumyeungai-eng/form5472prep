## What is actually being compared?

Form 5472 for a foreign-owned US disregarded entity is not a standalone return. Per the [IRS Instructions for Form 5472](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i5472), the entity attaches Form 5472 to a **pro forma Form 1120**, writes "Foreign-owned U.S. DE" across the top of page 1, and **cannot file electronically**. The package goes by fax to 855-887-7737, an IRS fax line and not a phone number, or by mail to the IRS Ogden PIN Unit in Ogden, Utah.

So the question is not "can our staff fill in a form". It is whether your firm wants to own five capabilities per client, every year: fact collection, preparation against the current instructions, independent review, a signature from the person authorised to sign for the LLC, and a retained submission record. One failure there turns a compliant client into a penalty client.

## When does keeping Form 5472 in-house win?

In-house wins when four conditions hold together.

The firm already prepares Form 1120 returns. The pro forma route is unusual, but a team filing corporate returns understands the cover page, the signature question and the discipline of reconciling schedules. The DE variation is an increment, not a new practice line.

Staff are trained and the training is current. The instructions carry a revision date for a reason, and a preparer who worked on packages last season and has read this season's instructions is genuinely capable.

Annual volume keeps the knowledge alive. Competence here decays fast. A team doing this monthly retains the detail: which party goes in Part I, why Part V needs a described statement for contributions and distributions, why line 1g's form count changes when a related party is added.

The firm retains its own submission evidence. A practice with a retention schedule and a place where the exact transmitted package and its fax report live together for years has the piece most small firms lack. Our [recordkeeping checklist](/blog/form-5472-recordkeeping-checklist) sets out what that file holds.

Where all four hold, outsourcing adds a handoff without adding capability. Keep it in-house.

Named cost lines, not invented numbers. We do not know your charge-out rates, and any figure printed here would be decoration. Cost each line with your firm's own rates over a full year, then compare that total against your expected filing count.

| Training and keeping current | Reading each revision, updating the procedure, onboarding new staff | Annual hours across everyone who touches the work, plus course cost |
| Fax infrastructure | A route that transmits at 300 DPI or higher and returns a retained report | Subscription or per-page cost, plus time to verify each transmission |
| Evidence retention | Storing the exact transmitted package and its report for years, retrievably | Storage and administration cost, plus the eventual retrieval |
| Error rework | Rebuilding and resubmitting a defective package, drafting the explanation | Your historical rework rate applied to your filing count |
| Deadline cover | A trained second person who can file when the first is unavailable | Training and keeping a backup current, used or not |

Two lines are routinely left out, and they are the two that hurt. **Error rework** is no rounding item: a defective package must be rebuilt in full, labelled as a correction, with an explanation identifying the original filing, as our guide to [correcting a filed Form 5472](/blog/amended-form-5472-correcting-errors) sets out. **Deadline cover** is fixed cost paid even in years it goes unused, the alternative being a missed deadline when one person is ill in early April.

## Why does review depth decide the risk?

Because an incomplete filing is treated as no filing. The IRS Instructions for Form 5472 state that a **$25,000** penalty may apply for failure to file when due and in the prescribed manner, for filing a substantially incomplete Form 5472, or for failure to maintain the required records. That penalty is not automatic for every mistake, and it applies per failure.

The consequence is specific: a cheap preparation with no independent review is not cheap. A complete, reconciled, reviewed package keeps a client out of the substantially-incomplete category, and review depth is the one factor where an under-resourced in-house process loses to both alternatives, a properly staffed in-house team and an outsourced specialist alike.

What a receipt proves deserves the same honesty. A provider fax report, ours or yours, evidences a transmission. It is not IRS acceptance of the return. The IRS states on its [delinquent international information return submission procedures](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/delinquent-international-information-return-submission-procedures) page that penalties may be assessed during processing without considering an attached reasonable cause statement.

## Which route should your firm pick?

Five questions about your firm as it is today, not as you intend it to be. The rule is strict: **the first no resolves the test.** There is no partial score and no tie.

| # | Question | If no |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Does your firm already prepare and file Form 1120 returns? | Outsource. The pro forma route is a poor first corporate return to learn on. |
| 2 | Will you file at least a dozen of these packages in the coming year? | Outsource. Below that, the knowledge goes stale between seasons. |
| 3 | Is there a named, currently trained preparer **and** a named trained backup? | Outsource. One-deep knowledge fails at the deadline. |
| 4 | Can you transmit at 300 DPI or higher and retain a report per filing? | Outsource. Evidence you cannot produce is not evidence. |
| 5 | Will a second person review every package against the current instructions before it is sent? | Outsource. Unreviewed packages carry the substantially-incomplete risk. |

**Five yes answers: keep the work in-house.** Your firm has the practice, the volume, the people, the transmission route and the review discipline; buying those from us would add a handoff without adding capability. That is a real outcome of this test, not a courtesy.

**Any no: outsource that work**, and run the test again next year if volume or staffing changes. A firm on four yes answers and one no usually finds it cheapest to outsource this season while closing the gap.

A mixed approach is legitimate. Some firms keep straightforward current-year filings and send the awkward ones out: multiple related parties, several unfiled past years, an owner with no US identifying number. That is allocation, not defeat.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is outsourcing cheaper than doing it in-house?

Not always. For a firm with a busy 1120 practice and steady volume, the marginal in-house cost per package can sit below $149. For a firm filing a handful a year, training and deadline cover dominate, and outsourcing wins.

### Does outsourcing transfer our professional liability?

No. Your obligations to your client are governed by your own engagement terms. We prepare and submit the package and a qualified tax accountant reviews it; we are not a CPA firm and do not give tax advice. Ask your insurer how your cover treats each route.

### Can we start outsourced and move in-house later?

Yes. Each filing is paid individually, with no subscription and no minimum, so there is nothing to unwind. Many firms outsource one season to see the finished package structure, then bring routine filings in-house once staff are trained.

### Who signs the filing if we outsource?

The person authorised to sign for the client LLC signs once, and the signature is embedded into every required box on the printable PDF. Your firm coordinates but does not sign for the client.

### Does the fax receipt prove the IRS accepted the return?

No. A fax receipt is transmission evidence only. It shows a package was sent to the IRS fax line, not that the IRS processed, matched or accepted the return. Retain it with your copy of the exact package.
