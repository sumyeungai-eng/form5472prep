Form 5472 line 1c asks for total assets, not revenue or profit. For a foreign-owned U.S. disregarded LLC, the best-supported practical approach is to calculate the LLC's tax-year-end book assets and use that figure; use zero only when the calculation genuinely produces zero. Do not infer that line 1c is blank or zero simply because the pro forma Form 1120 cover omits assets. This is a reading of two linked instructions, not a separate IRS rule written specifically for that situation. [IRS line 1c instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i5472), [IRS item D instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1120).

## Why do the two forms seem to disagree?

The Form 5472 instructions direct domestic reporting corporations to the asset figure in Form 1120, page 1, item D. Separately, their special foreign-owned U.S. DE provision limits the cover's required information to the entity's name, address, and items B and E. Item D is absent from that list. [IRS instructions, “Line 1c” and “Foreign-owned U.S. DEs”](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i5472).

The distinction is between an asset calculation and which cover fields are required. The special provision does not expressly tell these LLCs to omit line 1c on Form 5472. If a preparer also enters assets on the cover for consistency, that should be described as a preparation choice—not an additional express requirement in the special provision.

Our [pro forma 1120 guide](/blog/pro-forma-form-1120-foreign-owned-llc) explains the limited cover. An asset workpaper does not mean you must populate Schedule L for this package.

## What does “total assets” measure?

Item D uses assets at the end of the tax year, determined under the accounting method regularly used for the entity's books and records. It is not automatically fair market value or federal tax basis. For a calendar-year 2025 example, the measurement date is December 31, 2025—not the date you prepare the filing in 2026. A different tax-year end needs a different measurement date. [IRS Form 1120 instructions, item D](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1120).

Keep these concepts separate:

| Figure | What it describes |
|---|---|
| Total assets | Recorded assets held at the measurement date |
| Revenue and profit | Business activity and results over a period |
| Owner contributions | Funding events; not necessarily assets still held |
| Liabilities | Amounts the LLC owes |
| Net equity | Assets minus liabilities, not total assets |

For example, borrowing USD 4,000 can leave USD 4,000 in cash and a USD 4,000 debt. The debt does not erase the cash from total assets. The asset and liability sections are separate on [Form 1120 Schedule L](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f1120.pdf#page=6).

Likewise, line 1c is not a substitute for the [related-party totals on lines 1f, 1g, and 1h](/blog/form-5472-lines-1f-1g-1h-total-value).

## Which assets should you check?

Use the books as the starting point, then reconcile the supporting records. Schedule L's categories are a useful reference for what an asset total can contain, even when that schedule is not part of your pro forma filing. [Form 1120, Schedule L](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f1120.pdf#page=6).

| Category | Workpaper check |
|---|---|
| Cash and account balances | Reconcile every LLC account; avoid counting an in-transit transfer twice |
| Receivables | Use amounts recognized under the books' method, including applicable allowances |
| Inventory | Use the recorded year-end value, not anticipated selling proceeds |
| Equipment | Use recorded cost less accumulated book depreciation |
| Loans receivable and other assets | Check outstanding recorded balances, including loans made by the LLC |

A loan **to** the LLC belongs on the liability side; a recoverable loan **made by** the LLC can be an asset. Personally owned property is not automatically an LLC asset just because its owner uses it for work.

For non-USD balances, keep the original currency, valuation date, conversion method, rate source, and USD result. Do not automatically reuse an annual transaction-average rate for a year-end asset balance. Ask the preparer how the books' currency treatment supports the figure; our [exchange-rate guide](/blog/form-5472-currency-conversion-exchange-rates) explains the supporting records.

## Hypothetical example 1: cash-only service LLC

Assume a calendar-year LLC uses cash-basis books and, on December 31, 2025, has no assets other than the two reconciled USD balances below. There are no receivables recognized in its books, capitalized equipment, prepaid assets, or unrecorded balances. All values are hypothetical.

| Asset at December 31, 2025 | Book value, USD |
|---|---:|
| Business checking account | 2,400 |
| Separate business payment-account balance | 600 |
| **Total assets** | **3,000** |

Calculation: **USD 2,400 + USD 600 = USD 3,000**. The payment balance has not already been included in checking. Cash has no depreciation adjustment, so these reconciled balances are their book values.

Suppose the owner contributed USD 8,000 earlier that year. That historical contribution does not replace the USD 3,000 asset result. Money spent or withdrawn is not still cash at year-end. Neither the original contribution nor annual customer receipts should be copied into line 1c as “assets.”

## Hypothetical example 2: noncash assets and an owner loan

Assume a different calendar-year LLC maintains accrual-basis books in USD. Its December 31, 2025 records support the following values; it has no other assets, and the receivables require no bad-debt allowance. These are hypothetical book values, not market appraisals or tax-depreciation calculations.

| Asset at December 31, 2025 | Net book value, USD |
|---|---:|
| Cash | 1,000 |
| Customer receivables | 2,500 |
| Inventory | 3,000 |
| Equipment: 6,000 cost less 2,000 accumulated book depreciation | 4,000 |
| **Total assets** | **10,500** |

Calculation: **USD 1,000 + USD 2,500 + USD 3,000 + (USD 6,000 − USD 2,000) = USD 10,500**.

If the LLC also owes its owner USD 7,000, total assets remain USD 10,500. Assuming no other liabilities, equity is USD 3,500. Reporting USD 3,500 on line 1c would confuse equity with assets. Reporting only the USD 1,000 bank balance would omit USD 9,500 of noncash assets.

## What if last year's preparer left line 1c blank?

Ask for the filed package, year-end asset workpaper, and the reason for the omission. Check whether it was a deliberate interpretation of the pro forma cross-reference, a records gap, or an oversight.

Do not replace an unknown amount with zero. Reconstruct the correct tax-year-end position before deciding whether a correction is appropriate. If a material omission remains, have a qualified U.S. tax professional review the package and correction route. A blank field alone does not establish what the IRS received, how it processed the filing, or whether a penalty applies.

For a new filing, bring the reconciled balances and accounting assumptions into the preparation process. [Start your Form 5472 filing](/start); unresolved accounting or prior-filing questions should be settled with appropriate professional advice.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I enter zero if the LLC's bank account is empty?

Only if its total recorded assets are genuinely zero. Check other accounts, payment balances, inventory, equipment, and receivables recognized under its accounting method. An empty bank account is one input, not the conclusion.

### Is Form 5472 line 1c my annual revenue?

No. Revenue covers activity during a period; assets describe the year-end position. A business can have substantial revenue but little remaining cash, or no revenue but assets funded by its owner.

### Must pro forma 1120 item D match Form 5472 line 1c?

If you populate both as the same year-end asset figure, reconcile them. But item D is not among the special provision's required cover fields. Leaving that cover field blank does not establish that the LLC has zero assets or resolve what to enter on Form 5472.
