**The $25,000 penalty attaches to two separate failures: not filing Form 5472, and not maintaining the records the regulation requires. A perfectly prepared form sitting above an empty folder is still exposed. Treas. Reg. § 1.6038A-3 says what to keep, and the Form 5472 instructions tie the same penalty to keeping it.**

Most owners of a foreign-owned US LLC treat Form 5472 as a filing problem. File the form, keep the fax receipt, done. The regulation behind the form treats it as a records problem with a filing attached, and the penalty text in the instructions follows the regulation rather than the form.

[We prepare and fax the complete package from $149](/start), and the first thing we ask for is the documents behind the numbers — because those are what the regulation makes you keep.

## Does the $25,000 penalty really apply to records as well as to the form?

Yes. The [IRS Instructions for Form 5472](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i5472) state: "A penalty of $25,000 will be assessed on any reporting corporation that fails to file Form 5472 when due and in the manner prescribed. The penalty also applies for failure to maintain records as required by Regulations section 1.6038A-3."

Two sentences, two failures, one amount. The second sentence is the one almost nobody reads. It means the $25,000 is not a late-filing fee — it is the penalty for not meeting the § 6038A regime, and the regime has a records half.

The instructions also set out what happens when a failure is not cured: "If the failure continues for more than 90 days after notification by the IRS, an additional penalty of $25,000 will apply. This penalty applies with respect to each related party for which a failure occurs for each 30-day period (or part of a 30-day period) during which the failure continues after the 90-day period ends." The instructions add that "Criminal penalties under sections 7203, 7206, and 7207 may also apply for failure to submit information or for filing false or fraudulent information."

A notice that has already been assessed is a different problem with its own response window, covered in [what to do about a Form 5472 penalty notice](/blog/form-5472-penalty-notice-what-to-do).

## What does Treas. Reg. § 1.6038A-3 actually require you to keep?

It requires permanent books and records sufficient to establish the correctness of the return, including anything relevant to how related-party transactions should be treated. Paragraph (a)(1) requires a reporting corporation to "keep the permanent books of account or records as required by section 6001 that are sufficient to establish the correctness of the federal income tax return of the corporation," including records "to the extent they may be relevant to determine the correct U.S. tax treatment of transactions with related parties."

Note the standard. It is not a list of forms to fill in. It is a sufficiency test: could a reviewer, working only from the file, establish that the reported treatment was right?

The regulation then offers a safe harbor in paragraph (a)(2): "A corporation that maintains or causes another person to maintain the records listed in paragraph (c)(2) of this section that may be relevant to its foreign related party transactions and to its business or industry will be deemed to have met the record maintenance requirements of section 6038A."

Paragraph (c)(2) lists six categories. The qualifier "that may be relevant to its foreign related party transactions and to its business or industry" does real work for a one-person LLC: several categories were drafted with large multinational groups in mind and will be thin or empty for a single-member LLC whose only related party is its owner.

| § 1.6038A-3(c)(2) category | Regulation's own wording | What it looks like for a one-owner LLC |
|---|---|---|
| (i) Original entry books and transaction records | "general ledgers, sales journals, purchase order books, cash receipts books, cash disbursement books, canceled checks and bank statements, workpapers, sales contracts, and purchase invoices" | Bank statements, bookkeeping ledger, invoices, receipts |
| (ii) Profit and loss statements | Records enabling compilation of "material profit and loss statements of the reporting corporation and all related parties" | A simple annual P&L for the LLC |
| (iii) Pricing documents | "all documents relevant to establishing the appropriate price or rate for transactions between the reporting corporation and any foreign related party" | Support for what the LLC paid the owner, or an owner-controlled company, for services |
| (iv) Foreign country and third party filings | "financial and other documents relevant to transactions … filed with or prepared for any foreign government entity" | Home-country filings that describe the same transfers |
| (v) Ownership and capital structure records | "records or charts showing the relationship between the reporting corporation and the foreign related party" and a "worldwide organization chart" | A one-page chart naming the owner, the LLC, and any other company the owner controls |
| (vi) Loans, services and other transactions | "relevant documents relating to loans … guarantees … hedging arrangements … research and development expense allocations … service transactions" | Owner-loan notes, service agreements, interest schedules |

## Can a small LLC use the small-corporation exception to skip the records rule?

No — and this is the trap. [Treas. Reg. § 1.6038A-1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.6038A-1) does contain two escapes from the record-maintenance rules, and both expressly exclude the kind of entity a foreign-owned US LLC is.

The small corporation exception in paragraph (h) reads: "A reporting corporation (other than an entity that is a reporting corporation as a result of being treated as a corporation under § 301.7701-2(c)(2)(vi) of this chapter) that has less than $10,000,000 in U.S. gross receipts for a taxable year is not subject to §§ 1.6038A-3 and 1.6038A-5 for that taxable year."

The de minimis exception in paragraph (i) carries the identical parenthetical, and applies where gross related-party payments are "not more than $5,000,000 and is less than 10 percent of its U.S. gross income."

That parenthetical is the whole story. Section 301.7701-2(c)(2)(vi), headed "Special rule for reporting under section 6038A," is precisely the provision that treats a domestic disregarded entity solely owned by one foreign person as a separate corporation for § 6038A purposes. It is the reason the LLC files Form 5472 at all. So the very rule that pulls a foreign-owned US LLC into the regime is the rule that disqualifies it from the small-corporation and de minimis relief.

Plain reading: a dormant LLC with $0 of gross receipts gets no size-based pass on § 1.6038A-3. Both exceptions also confirm that an exempt corporation "remains subject to the information reporting requirements of § 1.6038A-2 and the general record maintenance requirements of section 6001" — so no reading of either paragraph leaves anyone with no records duty at all.

## Which document evidences which reportable transaction?

Match the document to the transaction type, because a bank line proves cash moved and almost nothing else. The table below maps the related-party movements a foreign-owned LLC actually has to the documents that evidence them, and to the feature that makes each one hold up. Audit your own folder against the middle column.

| Reportable transaction | Documents that evidence it | What makes it adequate |
|---|---|---|
| Owner sends money into the LLC as a contribution | LLC bank statement line showing the credit; the owner's own sending-account statement or wire confirmation; a dated owner resolution or written memo classifying it as contributed capital | Both sides of the same movement at the same date and amount, plus a contemporaneous statement of intent. A credit alone does not show who sent it or why |
| Owner lends money to the LLC | Signed loan note stating date, principal, interest rate or an express no-interest term, and repayment terms; bank lines for the advance | A note dated at or before the advance, with terms the later payments actually follow |
| LLC repays owner-loan principal | Bank statement line; a running loan-balance schedule tied to the note | Principal separated from interest, with the balance after each payment reconciling to the note |
| LLC distributes money to the owner | LLC bank statement line; ledger entry or owner resolution classifying it as a distribution | Classification recorded at the time of payment, not reconstructed later. "Transfer" is not a classification |
| Owner pays an LLC cost on a personal card | Vendor invoice or receipt issued in the LLC's name; the owner's card statement line; an expense report linking the two; the reimbursement bank line if reimbursed | The invoice names the LLC while the card names the owner — the pair is the proof. Reimbursements carry the expense-report reference |
| LLC pays the owner for services | Invoice describing the work and the period; a written services agreement; bank line; support for the rate | An invoice that describes services actually performed, and an agreement predating the invoice — the regulation's (c)(2)(iii) pricing documents |
| LLC pays a foreign company the owner controls | Invoice; intercompany agreement; ownership chart showing common control | The chart that proves the counterparty is a related party, not a vendor |
| Owner contributes property in kind (equipment, a domain, software) | Assignment or transfer document; valuation support; asset-register entry | A dated valuation with its method stated, not a round number |
| Any of the above denominated in a non-USD currency | An exchange-rate schedule recording the rate used, its published source, and the date applied | A named source and one consistent method across the year — see [currency conversion and exchange rates](/blog/form-5472-currency-conversion-exchange-rates) |

Owner loans, contributions and reimbursements are the three that get mislabelled most often; the distinctions are set out in [owner loans, contributions and reimbursements](/blog/form-5472-owner-loans-contributions-reimbursements).

## How long does § 1.6038A-3 say records must be kept?

The regulation gives no number of years. Paragraph (g), headed "Period of retention," states that records "shall be kept as long as they may be relevant or material to determining the correct tax treatment of any transaction between the reporting corporation and a related party, but in no case less than the applicable statute of limitations on assessment and collection with respect to the taxable year in which the transaction or item to which the records relate affects the U.S. tax liability of the reporting corporation."

So it is a relevance test with a floor, not a fixed term. Two consequences follow for a small LLC:

- A document stays required while it is still material. An owner loan advanced in 2022 and repaid in 2029 keeps its note relevant across every one of those years.
- The floor moves with the statute of limitations for the year the item affected US tax liability, which is a question about your own years. We do not compute a limitations period for anyone, and a qualified adviser should set the LLC's retention policy on its actual facts.

Treat the practical answer as "keep it while it can still matter, and do not throw out a loan note because the year it started is old."

## What happens when the records are outside the United States?

The regulation's default is that records be held in the US, with an express alternative for records kept abroad — the normal position for a non-resident owner. Paragraph (f)(1) provides that records "must be maintained within the United States, unless the conditions described in paragraph (f)(2) of this section are met," and (f)(2) lets a reporting corporation "maintain outside the United States records not ordinarily maintained in the United States."

The conditions are deadlines that start when the IRS asks. Reading paragraph (f)(2), a corporation keeping records abroad must do one of the following after a request by the Service:

1. **Deliver** the original documents or duplicates requested within **60 days** of the request, and provide translations of specific documents within **30 days** of a translation request; or
2. **Move** the originals or duplicates to the United States within **60 days** of the request, provide an index to the records plus the name and address of a US custodian having control over them and the US address where they are held within **60 days**, and continue to maintain them in the United States.

For material profit and loss statements, 120 days substitutes for 60 days. Paragraph (e) separately allows agreements with the District Director covering "the records the reporting corporation must maintain or cause another to maintain, how the records must be maintained, the period of retention for the records, and by whom the records must be maintained."

Sixty days is the figure to plan around. A folder that is complete, named and in one place is answerable in an afternoon. A folder that has to be rebuilt from three banks and two dead payment platforms is not, and the clock does not pause while anyone searches.

## What should you do before any request arrives?

Build the file in the same year the transactions happen, then leave it alone. A short routine, run once at year end:

1. List every movement between the LLC and the owner, or the LLC and any company the owner controls, for the year.
2. Classify each one — contribution, distribution, loan advance, loan repayment, owner-paid cost, payment for services — and write the classification down where the entry lives.
3. Attach the document from the middle column of the table above to each classified entry, and name the file so the entry and the document find each other.
4. Record the exchange rate, its source and the date for anything not in USD.
5. Save the filed package and its delivery evidence with that year's folder, not somewhere else.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can the IRS penalise me if I filed Form 5472 correctly but kept no records?

The instructions state the $25,000 penalty "also applies for failure to maintain records as required by Regulations section 1.6038A-3." Whether it is asserted in a given case depends on facts we cannot assess. The exposure is separate from the filing exposure.

### Does § 1.6038A-3 name the number of years to keep records?

No. Paragraph (g) requires records be kept as long as they may be relevant or material to a related-party transaction, and in no case less than the applicable statute of limitations for the year affected. No fixed term is stated.

### My LLC earns well under $10 million — does the small corporation exception help?

No. The exception in § 1.6038A-1(h) excludes entities that are reporting corporations because of § 301.7701-2(c)(2)(vi), which is the rule that makes a foreign-owned US disregarded entity a reporting corporation in the first place.

### Are bank statements enough on their own?

Generally no. A statement line evidences that cash moved on a date for an amount. It usually does not evidence the counterparty's identity or the character of the transfer, which is what related-party treatment turns on.

### Must the records be held in the United States?

Paragraph (f)(1) sets US maintenance as the default. Paragraph (f)(2) permits records not ordinarily maintained in the US to be held abroad, provided they are delivered or moved to the US within 60 days of an IRS request, with 30 days for translations.

### Do I need records for ordinary customer sales too?

Keep ordinary accounting records for all business activity under section 6001. Customer and platform revenue is not a reportable transaction on Form 5472, so identify the related-party items separately within the same books.
